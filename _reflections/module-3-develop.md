Module 3 pushed me to think less about collecting theories and more about what actually changes when I design. Across media, online learning, and AI, the shift was similar: I used to treat each topic as a set of rules. I now see them as ways of understanding how people encounter what I build—and as reminders that I am still the one who decides what fits our learners.

---

## 1. Media development

### What shifted in my thinking

I used to treat educational media as something that should look polished. After reading Guo, Kim, and Rubin (2014) and Brame (2016), and revisiting Mayer’s multimedia principles, I started paying attention to how people actually stay with media. Guo and colleagues studied millions of edX video sessions and found that shorter videos held attention better—median engagement stayed around six minutes even when videos were longer—and that informal talking-head styles often worked better than high-production lecture recordings. Brame frames effective video around managing cognitive load, supporting engagement, and promoting active learning. Mayer’s principles made the same point differently: words and visuals should work together, extra decoration can get in the way, and signaling what matters helps people notice the right things. “Good media,” for me, is less about looking finished and more about whether the design asks for useful thinking.

### How it shapes my design decisions

On our Digital Accessibility pilot, I am the UX researcher / visual designer. We are building a Canvas training module for Faculty Coordinators and staff—not a long lecture series. I want the pages and any short media we add to stay focused: what accessibility is, why it matters (including student feedback), a checklist, and tool-specific resources for Google Docs, Slides, and Canvas that people can open while they work. When I lay out a screen, I ask whether each visual carries meaning or just fills space, and whether the next step is clear enough to act on without a long explanation. I am still the one deciding what belongs on the page for busy course staff.

## 2. Online learning

### What shifted in my thinking

One idea that stayed with me is that **constraints are good, up to a point**. I used to hear “constraint” as a limit that makes a design feel smaller. I now hear it as a design tool: a clear boundary can help people know where to start. The Community of Inquiry model helped me see online learning as more than content delivery—it depends on teaching, social, and cognitive presence working together. Karen Costa’s extension, including emotional and regulatory presence, pushed me further. Presence is not only about discussion forums; it is also about whether people feel steady enough, and guided enough, to keep going when the work is unfamiliar. The limit matters too: the wrong constraints can block participation instead of supporting it.

### How it shapes my design decisions

Our learners are FCs and teaching-team staff who often feel unsure where to begin with accessibility. The pilot leans into useful constraints: a clear path (basics → checklist while building → Cidi Labs and UDOIT), scaffolding by accessibility challenge, and resources next to the tools people already use. That is presence in a practical form—structure that reduces “where do I start?” without pretending one checklist solves every course. I keep asking whether a constraint helps someone take a next step, or only makes the module feel narrower. If a step creates confusion rather than clarity, it is my job to revise it before we treat the pilot as finished.

## 3. AI and learning design

### What shifted in my thinking

Dede and McCool’s framing of intelligence augmentation (IA) changed how I relate to AI tools. The useful distinction is between **reckoning**—calculative prediction, pattern-finding, fast generation from what is already known—and **judgment**—practical wisdom shaped by context, values, and lived experience. AI can support reckoning; humans still hold judgment. That also made cognitive offloading feel less abstract: handing too much thinking to a tool can leave important decisions unexamined. Simulations matter in their account of upskilling because they let people practice judgment before the stakes are real. I no longer ask only whether AI can produce something faster. I ask which part of the work is reckoning, and which part still needs human judgment.

### How it shapes my design decisions

In our accessibility project, tools like UDOIT or Cidi Labs can help surface issues in a Canvas site—closer to reckoning. Deciding what matters for a specific course, how to talk about student impact, and what to prioritize for busy staff still needs judgment from people who know the context. When I use AI in my own design process, I use it to draft options, organize notes, or explore layout ideas. I do not treat its output as the final voice of the module. I remain responsible for whether the training feels usable, respectful, and accurate for HGSE staff. AI can widen what I can try; I stay accountable for what we ship in the pilot and for what we change after feedback before the Gallery Walk.
