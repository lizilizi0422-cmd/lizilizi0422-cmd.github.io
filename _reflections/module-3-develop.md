## 1. Media development

I see effective media as a way to protect learners' limited attention. Attention runs out quickly: Guo et al. (2014) found that viewers in edX courses typically stopped engaging after about six minutes, regardless of video length. Extra content also competes with the core idea. Mayer's (2009) coherence principle explains that working memory is limited, so unnecessary images, sounds, and tangents take up space the main message needs. Building on this, Brame (2016) recommends short segments, clear signals for key ideas, and cutting extraneous elements. Engagement also grows when learners act, which is why Brame (2016) suggests guiding questions and embedded prompts.

When I bring video into an online class, I would start by asking what each video needs to teach and cut everything that does not serve that goal. I would break longer lectures into short segments, each focused on one idea, and use simple on-screen cues to point learners to what matters most. Equally important, I would build in moments of interaction. A video can pause to ask learners a question, invite a short written reflection, or give them a chance to respond to the speaker. These small moments turn watching into doing, help learners stay focused, and make an online class feel closer to a real conversation with the person teaching.

## 2. Online learning

Good online structure guides learners and leaves them room to choose. Structure is itself a form of teaching. Garrison et al. (2000) include selecting and organizing content in teaching presence, which shows that a clear course design tells learners what matters and how ideas connect. Learners also need support to manage their own learning. Costa (2022) adds regulatory presence, which helps learners recognize what they need and work toward it, especially those whose executive function is affected by stress, trauma, or neurodivergence. Since Costa (2022) notes that this support does not aim for a single uniform result, one fixed path cannot fit every learner.

My approach is to design a recommended path that gives newcomers a clear place to begin, while also offering several entry points for learners who arrive with a specific need. I would organize content around the questions learners actually bring, so someone looking for one answer can find it without working through everything before it. I would also give learners simple tools to plan and check their own progress, such as a checklist or short self-assessment, so they can see where they are and decide what to do next. This kind of design respects different paces, needs, and ways of using technology, and it helps learners become more independent over time.

## 3. AI and learning design

In my view, we should embrace AI and keep decision-making authority with people, which makes human judgment and creativity more important. AI and humans bring different strengths. Dede and McCool (2024) describe AI's strength as reckoning, calculative prediction from data, and the human strength as judgment drawn from experience, ethics, and empathy. Judgment is the part AI cannot replace: Dede et al. (2021) define it as ethical, context-appropriate deliberation and argue that AI struggles with the social cues and ambiguity it requires. Human authority only matters when people understand a tool's limits. An accessibility checker can flag a missing alt attribute, but only a person can decide whether the alt text conveys what an image teaches.

In my own design work, I use AI as a partner for reckoning. It helps me generate options, draft layouts, and spot patterns quickly. The decisions that shape the learning experience stay with me: which option fits the learners, what tone is appropriate, and whether the content truly teaches what it should. I check AI output against what I know about the learners and the goals before anything reaches them. When I design learning experiences that involve AI, I would make this division clear to learners too, showing them what the tool can handle and where their own judgment and creativity are needed. Learners who practice judgment alongside AI are better prepared to use it well.
