<p class="r-intro">Module 2 reflection on educational design, learners, equity, and assessment.</p>

I tended to associate design with visual elements and art. I now understand design more broadly as a purposeful practice of solving problems. In the education field, it means deciding what matters, anticipating how learners with different experiences will encounter the learning environment, identifying barriers, creating opportunities for participation, and using evidence to revise what has been designed.

## Course Design as a System for Learning

<div class="r-box" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">What course design is</p>
Course design is the intentional organisation of learning goals, content, learning experiences, interactions, resources, and assessment into a coherent learning experience. It involves more than arranging activities in sequence. It requires educators to consider how these elements work together to support access, participation, and the demonstration of understanding. Course design therefore creates pathways into learning rather than simply delivering content.
</div>

<p class="r-label">Design models that shaped my thinking</p>
**ADDIE** provides a systematic cycle for planning, implementing, and evaluating learning experiences. **Backward Design** begins with the intended learning results and evidence before planning activities. The **5Ds framework** places greater emphasis on learner needs, context, delivery, and reflection. Together, these models helped me understand course design as a structured but iterative process.

<figure class="r-fig"><img src="/images/reflections/module-2/addie.png" alt="ADDIE cycle diagram: Analyze, Design, Development and Implement connected by revision arrows around a central Evaluation box" loading="lazy"><figcaption><b>Figure 1.</b> ADDIE as a cyclical design model. Reproduced from the Design Models Fall26 course materials.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="r-mine" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">Understanding and application</p>
These models helped me recognize accessibility as part of course design from the beginning. Accessibility means that children can understand content, engage with resources and peers, participate in activities, and demonstrate learning in different ways. This understanding relates to Universal Design for Learning, which supports multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression.

When planning a learning experience, I would consider whether its language and resources are accessible to children from different linguistic and cultural backgrounds. Bilingual materials, visual explanations, and supplementary videos or books in children’s home languages could reduce language barriers. I would also consider disability and neurodiversity. For a child with ADHD who needs support with attention or organization, I could present materials clearly, divide activities into manageable stages, use a visual schedule, and include movement breaks.

Children could demonstrate their understanding through speech, drawing, construction, gesture, or AAC. These options preserve the learning goal while reducing barriers to participation and expression. Observing how children participate would help me identify remaining barriers and revise the learning experience.
</div>
</section>

## Learner-Centred Design as Understanding Before Designing

<div class="r-box" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">What learner-centred design is</p>
Learner-centred design begins with an understanding of the people who will experience the learning. It considers learners’ motivations, prior experiences, emotions, and barriers to participation. Design decisions are therefore based on evidence of how learners experience the environment, rather than assumptions about an average learner.
</div>

<p class="r-label">Design models that shaped my thinking</p>
**Human-centred design** and **design thinking** provide a process of empathising, defining, ideating, prototyping, and testing. Empathy is developed through observation, conversation, and feedback. **Learner analysis** and **participatory design** extend this process by allowing learners’ experiences and perspectives to inform design decisions.

<figure class="r-fig"><img src="/images/reflections/module-2/design-thinking.jpg" alt="Five colored hexagons in a row reading Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype and Test, from the Stanford d.school" loading="lazy"><figcaption><b>Figure 2.</b> The five modes of design thinking. Adapted from <a href="https://dschool.stanford.edu/tools/design-thinking-bootleg">Design Thinking Bootleg</a>, Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford University, 2018.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="r-mine" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">Understanding and application</p>
I now understand learner-centred design as an evidence-based approach to educational decision-making. It requires humility because an educator’s initial interpretation of a child’s behaviour may be incomplete. Continued observation and revision are therefore necessary to ensure that design decisions reflect the learner’s experience rather than the educator’s assumptions.

In practice, when designing for children, we should observe how they use materials, respond to questions, communicate with peers, and move through routines before deciding what to change. If a child avoids a group activity, for example, this should not automatically be interpreted as a lack of interest. Possible explanations include the group being too large, unclear instructions, or the need for another form of participation that better supports the child’s language or social-emotional development. A different prompt could then be prototyped and its effect observed.
</div>
</section>

## Equity and Accessibility as Conditions for Participation

<div class="r-box" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">What equity and accessibility mean in design</p>
Designing for equity and accessibility means creating learning conditions in which diverse learners can access, participate in, and demonstrate learning without having to overcome unnecessary barriers. Equal treatment does not always produce equitable participation because learners differ in ability, language, culture, or prior experience. A design that assumes one normal way of communicating or showing knowledge can exclude learners even when everyone receives the same materials.
</div>

<p class="r-label">Theories and models used</p>
The **disability-identity** materials helped me understand disability as human diversity, identity, community, and culture rather than only as an individual deficit. **Universal Design for Learning** provides a practical framework by encouraging multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression. The discussion of **adult learning** and **competency-based education** also showed that flexibility can support access, but flexibility alone does not guarantee equity unless learners also receive meaningful support.

<figure class="r-fig"><img src="/images/reflections/module-2/udl-guidelines.jpg" alt="CAST Universal Design for Learning Guidelines chart with three columns: multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression, across access, support and executive function rows" loading="lazy"><figcaption><b>Figure 3.</b> Universal Design for Learning: multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression. Reproduced from the Equity 2026 course materials and based on the <a href="https://udlguidelines.cast.org/">CAST UDL Guidelines</a>.</figcaption></figure>

<div class="r-mine" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">Understanding and application</p>
I understand equity as a design question about participation and power: Who is expected to fit the environment, whose needs were considered at the beginning, and who has influence over decisions? The goal is to vary the routes through which they can engage, communicate, and show their developing understanding based on different needs.

Applying equity and accessibility begins with identifying where access breaks down. First, systematic observation helps distinguish an accessibility barrier from a lack of ability or interest. The educator examines when participation decreases and whether the difficulty is related to the resource, environment, or communication. Second, guided questions and scaffolding help clarify the child’s experience while providing temporary support. This reduces reliance on adult assumptions and reveals what type of adjustment may be needed. Finally, the evidence gathered informs a targeted change to the learning environment. The child’s response is then observed to determine whether access has improved. If the barrier remains, the design requires further revision. Accessibility is therefore an evidence-based cycle of design.
</div>
</section>

## Authentic Assessment as Evidence of Meaningful Learning

<div class="r-box" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">What authentic assessment is</p>
Authentic assessment asks learners to use knowledge and skills in meaningful or realistic situations. It is not limited to recalling information. It makes visible the understanding, judgement, problem solving, communication, creativity, and reflection that matter in the domain.
</div>

<p class="r-label">Theories and models used</p>
**Cognitive Apprenticeship** provides a way to support authentic performance through modelling, coaching, scaffolding, reflection, and exploration. **Scaffolding** is temporary support that enables learners to engage in a complex task before they can perform it independently; the support should be adjusted or gradually faded as competence develops. **Formative assessment** and feedback are central because learners need opportunities to improve while the learning is still in progress.

<div class="r-mine" markdown="1">
<p class="r-label">Understanding and application</p>
I now understand assessment as a design decision that communicates what counts as learning. If I only assess recall, I may miss children’s developing ability to explain ideas to a new situation. Authentic assessment gives educators richer evidence of development because it attends to the process as well as the final product.

In daily education, authentic assessment can be embedded in play, storytelling, or collaborative inquiry. For example, when asking a child to define balance, the educator could invite the child to build a bridge and explain why it stands or falls. Hands-on modeling, open-ended questions, and temporary visual support could guide the child before the support is gradually withdrawn. The evidence would include the child’s building process, like reasoning, persistence, and interaction, etc, not only whether the final bridge stands.
</div>
